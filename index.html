Louis James Vasseur

MSc Computer Science EPFL

I work on machine learning for scientific measurements and interactive systems. I’m particularly interested in how models can recover useful structure from observations that are incomplete, noisy, or spread across several different kinds of data.

I’m specialising in AI and Data Science at EPFL and lead the Research Track of the EPFL AI Team. My work moves between molecular science, neuroscience and scientific simulation, alongside a growing interest in the data systems that support machine learning.

01How my work has developed

My early work at Numenta involved benchmarking language-model inference: understanding how models behaved across tasks and computing environments. At EPFL, leading GESTURE brought me closer to building the whole interaction. We were taking models trained on recorded signing and making them respond to a person in front of a camera. The quality of that experience depended on how data reached the model, how predictions were interpreted, and how the pieces worked together.

A team project on galaxy-cluster mass maps brought a different kind of responsibility. The generated image represented a physical system. Its appearance could be convincing while its distribution of mass was wrong. That pushed me to think more carefully about what a learned representation retains, and what it needs to retain for the task we actually care about.

I pursued that question in molecular science. I proposed STRUCTURE in the AI Team and continued it during a research internship in EPFL’s LIAC lab. Our first spectral model could identify molecules among candidates; my work on TAG-X focused on reconstructing the structure itself. This meant working through the connection between measurements, their representation, and the generator that uses them. It led to my first-author paper, accepted at a NeurIPS 2026 workshop.

02What I’m working towards

I’m now exploring that connection in BRAIN, which I lead with the EPFL NeuroAI Lab. Going from a recorded EEG dataset to someone wearing a cap makes the measurement system part of the model: timing, signal quality and adaptation determine what information the decoder receives. I enjoy being able to work on both sides, from learning experiments to the hardware and software needed to run them.

My work on surgical world models asks how we can assess whether generated motion responds meaningfully to an action. With EPFL DIAS, I’m studying learned indexes—a different setting where the usefulness of a prediction depends on the cost of the complete system. Across these projects, I’m interested in how representation, learning and computation should be designed together.

Longer term, I want to work on AI that can support scientific reasoning: combining measurements, proposing explanations, and using domain tools or experimental feedback to test them. Multimodal learning, structured generation and models that interact with their environment are the parts of that direction I’m most drawn to.

03Research as a team effort

As Research Lead, I help turn a partner lab’s question into a project a small student team can carry through a semester. Much of the job is deciding what needs to happen first, what would count as progress, and what the next team should inherit. I write and review research plans and help teams connect their individual contributions to a shared experiment.

Leading GESTURE and BRAIN has kept that work practical. In BRAIN this autumn, I organised the effort around recording quality, stimuli and adaptation, with the closed-loop extension as a stretch goal. I find this part of research rewarding: helping a team understand why a problem is difficult, then finding a sequence of achievable steps that still leaves room for ambitious ideas.

At AMLD 2026, I presented GESTURE and took part in an EPFL AI Team panel on applied AI for social impact.
